Retropharyngeal &
Parapharyngeal Space

Two potential spaces on either side of the pharynx, separated by a few millimetres of fascia — and yet one drains into the mediastinum while the other is the highway for salivary and neural tumours. This page maps both: fascial layers in cross-section, vertical extent, contents, and what goes wrong.

01 · Orientation

The 60-second mental model

3D in one sentence

1 Wrap, then stack

The pharynx is a muscular tube wrapped by the buccopharyngeal fascia. Directly behind it lies a thin loose connective-tissue plane — the retropharyngeal space (RPS). Behind that is the alar fascia, then the danger space, then prevertebral fascia draped over longus colli and the vertebral bodies.

So from airway lumen going back: mucosa → constrictor → buccopharyngeal → RPS → alar → danger space → prevertebral → muscle → bone.

2 Then go lateral

Move side-wards from the pharyngeal wall and you enter the parapharyngeal space (PPS) — a fat-filled inverted pyramid, one on each side, base on the skull base, apex down at the hyoid. Its medial wall is the pharynx itself, so anything expanding in it pushes the tonsil and lateral pharyngeal wall inward.

Think: RPS = the space behind the pharynx; PPS = the space beside the pharynx. Both communicate freely with each other and with the carotid sheath.

3 Gravity is the plot

Fascial planes are downward-conducting tubes. Infection does not respect the level of the pharynx — it follows the path of least resistance into the neck and chest. That is why a sore throat, a fish bone, or an infected molar becomes mediastinitis.

  • RPS → stops at T2… unless it ruptures through the alar fascia into the danger space.
  • Danger space → clear, midline, no lymphatics → runs the whole way to the diaphragm.
  • PPS → carotid sheath → mediastinum, or down to the submandibular space / up to the infratemporal fossa and orbit.

Why it is called “danger”. Grodinsky & Holyoke (1938) showed this interalar/prevertebral plane is a loose, avascular, midline sheath open from the skull base to the diaphragm — the fastest route to descending necrotizing mediastinitis (mortality 40–50 % if drainage is delayed).

Retropharyngeal space

Skull base → T1–T2. Contained because the alar fascia blends into the buccopharyngeal fascia inferiorly, and the midline raphe resists crossing. Expect a compartmentalised collection unless it ruptures posteriorly.

Level key: hyoid ≈ C3, cricoid ≈ C6, thoracic inlet ≈ T1, aortic arch ≈ T4, diaphragm ≈ T8–T12 on inspiration. Schematic spacing, real anatomy is lordotic.

Route A — “the slide”

RPA erodes posteriorly through the alar fascia → danger space → posterior mediastinum → empyema, pericarditis. Fast, midline, often out of proportion to the neck findings.

Route B — “the sleeve”

PPS or tonsillar infection → carotid sheath → mediastinum along the great vessels; plus septic IJV thrombophlebitis (Lemierre) with pulmonary emboli.

Route C — “around the corner”

PPS apex communicates with the submandibular space: dental origin infection goes floor-of-mouth → PPS (and back). Ludwig angina closes the airway from below.

PosteriorAlar fascia (a thin accessory split from the prevertebral layer)
LateralCarotid sheath; blends with the PPS laterally — no true fascial wall
SuperiorBase of skull: alar fascia splits from prevertebral at the petrous temporal bone
InferiorT1–T2, where alar fascia fuses with the buccopharyngeal fascia (some texts: T2)
ContentsLoose areolar tissue + fat; retropharyngeal lymph nodes of Rouvière (C1–C2/C3, lateral to midline, largest in infants, atrophy by ~4–5 y); small vessels from ascending pharyngeal artery; the median raphe
Continues as→ danger space (posteriorly) · → mediastinum via carotid sheath · → PPS laterally

Exam one-liner

“The retropharyngeal space lies between the buccopharyngeal and alar fascia, runs from skull base to T2, contains the nodes of Rouvière, and is bounded in the midline by the raphe.”

Common trap: many books say “RPS = between buccopharyngeal and prevertebral fascia”. That definition folds the danger space in and predicts disease down to the diaphragm — which is exactly the point of separating the alar layer. Know both conventions and state which one you are using.

Developmental point

In small children the RPS is a paired set of compartments divided by the vascular midline raphe; suppuration of the lateral nodes therefore makes a unilateral bulge. After the raphe atrophies, collections spread bilaterally and midline.

Rule of thumb: prestyloid mass → pushes the tonsil medially and may cause trismus; poststyloid mass → pushes the lateral wall posteromedially without trismus but with lower cranial nerve signs or a pulsatile, non-transilluminant mass. PosteriorPrevertebral fascia over longus colli
SuperiorSkull base — alar and prevertebral layers diverge at the petrous apex

Surgical implication: a contained RPS abscess can often be drained transorally. Once the alar fascia is breached, transoral drainage alone is not enough — re-image the chest.
05 · What lives there, what goes wrong

Contents → differential diagnosis

Retropharyngeal space pathology

Age / causeMechanism
Children 1–4 ySuppurative retropharyngeal lymphadenitis after URI / tonsillitis / otitis — nodes of Rouvière are largest here (retropharyngeal abscess, “RPA”)
AdultsPenetrating injury: fish/chicken bone, dental injection, intubation / TEE / endoscopy, trauma to posterior pharyngeal wall
Oesophageal perforationBoerhaave, tumour, foreign body → gas + saliva in the space, rapid mediastinal soiling
Cold abscessTB of C-spine (Pott) tracking through prevertebral/alar planes, minimal inflammation
Non-infectiousHaematoma (anticoagulation, trauma), lymphoma/nodal disease, oedema post-surgery, chordoma/retroversion mimic
  • Triad to remember: fever + dysphagia/odynophagia + stiff, extended neck (loss of cervical lordosis).
  • Drooling, muffled “hot-potato” voice, trismus if PPS involved, stridor = late & ominous.
  • Inspect the posterior pharyngeal wall: asymmetric bulge above the level of the tonsils is the giveaway.
  • Airway first. Nebulised adrenaline/antibiotics for small (≤2–2.5 cm) collections in stable children; I&D (transoral, head-down, suction ready) for large, airway-threatening or adult/foreign-body cases.

Parapharyngeal space pathology

CategoryTypical entities
Salivary (pre-styloid)Pleomorphic adenoma of deep parotid lobe — the most common PPS mass (~40–50 % of all PPS tumours); “dumbbell” through the stylomandibular tunnel; minor-salivary malignancy
Neurogenic (post-styloid)Schwannoma/neurofibroma (vagus, sympathetic), paraganglioma (vagal, carotid body, jugulare), meningocele
VascularICA aneurysm/pseudoaneurysm, IJV thrombosis, arteriovenous malformation, haemangioma/lymphatic malformation in children
InfectivePPS abscess from tonsillitis, peritonsillar abscess, infected 3rd molar, otitis/mastoid (Bezold), suppurative nodes; actinomycosis
Nodes / systemicMetastatic SCC (tonsil, base of tongue, nasopharynx), lymphoma, HIV/TB, branchial cyst (2nd), lipoma, chondroma of the styloid
  • Tumours: painless lateral neck swelling + medial bulge of the tonsillar fossa, soft palate pushed in, normal mucosa, slow growth over years.
  • Otologic cue: Eustachian tube compression → unilateral serous otitis media / aural fullness in an adult = image the nasopharynx and PPS.
  • Trismus = irritation of medial pterygoid/masticator space (usually prestyloid or infection).
  • Poststyloid nerve signs: hoarseness (X), dysphagia/aspiration & loss of gag (IX), shoulder drop (XI), tongue wasting/deviation (XII), miosis/ptosis — Horner (sympathetic chain); IX–XII combined = Vernet / Collet-Sicard syndromes.
  • Approach for benign prestyloid deep-lobe tumours: transcervical (± mandibulotomy), not transoral — capsular spillage and facial nerve risk.
Step 1 · Where is the bulge? — look in the mouth, feel the neck
Posterior pharyngeal wall, midline or paramedianThink RPS: RPA, haematoma, TB cold abscess. Check lateral neck film for prevertebral thickness & gas.
Lateral pharyngeal wall / tonsil pushed mediallyThink PPS: deep parotid or neural tumour if slow & painless; abscess if hot, trismus, toxic.
▼
Step 2 · Infection or tumour?
Acute, fever, pain, trismus, toxicAirway, blood cultures, IV antibiotics + contrast CT. Drain if ≥2.5 cm, gas, airway compromise, or adult/foreign-body cause. Then hunt for the source (bone, tooth, tonsil, oesophagus).
Slow, painless, firm, intact mucosaContrast CT ± MRI of skull base→hyoid. Do not incise or blindly biopsy a PPS mass. Vascular/symmetric markers: urinary/plasma metanephrines for paraganglioma; MRI for fat-rich pleomorphic adenoma.
▼
Step 3 · Has it left the space?
Chest pain, sepsis, pleural effusion, wide mediastinum on CXRDescending necrotizing mediastinitis until proven otherwise: CT neck + whole chest, early aggressive drainage (often thoracotomy/VATS), ICU, broad-spectrum cover. Mortality climbs steeply beyond 24–48 h.
Neck onlyTranscervical or transoral drainage of the involved space, keep drains, serial re-image; dental/ENT source control to stop recurrence.
06 · Bedside

Signs, syndromes & the traps

ward rounds

Retropharyngeal abscess — checklist

WhoPeak 2–4 y; adults after trauma/instrumentation
LookTorticollis, neck held extended, drooling, muffled voice, stridor
SeeAsymmetric bulge of posterior wall, usually paramedian in kids
FilmLoss of cervical lordosis; prevertebral soft tissue widening; gas
ScanContrast CT neck + upper chest, always including the mediastinum
Don'tDo not probe the airway in a child with stridor — theatre, ENT + anaesthetics, rigid suction, head-down position

PPS abscess — checklist

WhoOlder children/adults; dental (3rd molar), post-tonsillitis, “collar-stick” abscess
LookSevere trismus, firm tender angle-of-mandible swelling, medially displaced tonsil with flat palate (vs uvular deviation in PTA)
TrapLittle intra-oral swelling because the space is deep — “toxic patient, boring mouth”
ComplicationIJV thrombosis & septic pulmonary emboli (Lemierre), CN IX–XII palsy, carotid blowout, mediastinitis via carotid sheath
DrainTranscervical for most; transoral only for well-defined medial pointing collections

Red flags → act now

  • Stridor, tripod position, pooling of saliva
  • Neck swelling crossing the midline or tracking below the clavicle
  • Chest pain + fever + recent sore throat = mediastinitis workup
  • Lower cranial nerve palsies with a PPS mass
  • Diabetes, immunosuppression, or anticoagulation with any deep neck pain
  • Failure to improve on 48 h of IV antibiotics → re-image, don't switch and wait
Lateral neck radiograph · measuring prevertebral soft tissue

at C6, soft tissue ≤ the AP diameter of the vertebral body. Beware: cry, extension, supine & very young age all falsely thicken it. Gas, straightened lordosis, or a displaced posterior wall = CT now.

Named syndromes near the PPS

VernetJugular foramen: IX + X + XI
Collet-SicardIX–XII, no Horner (extracranial, poststyloid PPS)
VillaretIX–XII + Horner — retroparotid space
JacksonX, XI, XII — below the jugular foramen
LemierrePost-tonsillar Fusobacterium necrophorum septic thrombophlebitis of the IJV with pulmonary emboli
BezoldMastoid tip abscess tracking into the neck via SCM — a PPS look-alike

Mnemonic for the PPS differential

The “P” list, ordered by how often you'll meet them: Pleomorphic adenoma (deep parotid), Paraganglioma, Peripheral nerve sheath tumour (schwannoma/neurofibroma), Parotid rest/inflammatory, Pseudoaneurysm, Pus (abscess) — plus Poorly-differentiated nodal metastasis and Lymphoma as the ugly extras. Always exclude a vascular lesion before touching it.

Three sentences to say out loud when reading

1) Is the PPS fat still fat (dark on CT) or is it stranding/fluid? 2) Which side of the styloid line is the lesion, and does it splay the carotid–IJV bundle? 3) How far down does it go — did it cross the alar fascia into the danger space, and is the mediastinum clean?

Pitfalls

Pharyngeal constrictor asymmetry from swallowed air/peristalsis mimics asymmetry. Unopacified IJV looks like thrombus. A high-riding tonsil/lymphoid tissue bulges the lateral wall. Cervical oesophageal diverticulum or a retropharyngeal oedema in heart failure can look like an abscess. Children: a crying, extended neck falsely thickens the prevertebral band.

Reporting template

“Hypodense collection with peripheral enhancement centred in the right/left retropharyngeal space, x mm craniocaudal, from level to level. Alar fascia intact / breached. No danger-space fluid. Parapharyngeal fat planes preserved / effaced. Airway narrowed to x mm. Great vessels patent, IJV …. Lung bases/mediastinum …”

Draw-it test

Blank paper, 60 s: sagittal neck with the 3 fascial lines + the T2 fusion. Then axial neck with 2 fat wedges + styloid line + carotid sheath. If you can draw both, you own this topic.

Teach-back line

“Retropharyngeal = behind, parapharyngeal = beside. Behind stops at T2 unless it breaks through the alar fascia; beside is a fat pyramid that carries parotid tumours up to the skull base and nerve tumours to the carotid sheath.”

Where it is tested

ENT finals, FRCS ORL-HNS, radiology physics of neck spaces, MRCS Part A (deep neck infections), and every viva where you are asked “what is the danger space?”

Primary sources

Grodinsky & Holyoke, Am J Anat 1938 (interfascial planes); Levitt & Maran, J Laryngol Otol 1990 (PPS tumours); Papadakis et al., Head Neck 2001 (PPS lesions); Logan Jones et al. on descending necrotizing mediastinitis. See allam.pdf / Macleod_*.pdf in this repo for your own notes.
